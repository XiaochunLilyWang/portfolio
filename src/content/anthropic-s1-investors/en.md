The core problem isn't supply. It's demand.

A headlong year for AI is about to reach a grand and highly uncertain finale in mid-November: Anthropic's listing.

We invited Rob Li, a public-markets investor who founded Amont Partners in New York, and Lu Zhang, founding partner of Fusion Fund and a longtime venture investor in Silicon Valley, to take apart Anthropic's prospectus with us — and the wider changes in the AI industry behind it.

The filing still does not answer the market's central worry: is demand sustainable?

According to the S-1 Anthropic updated on 2 November, its total long-term compute commitments have reached $895.4 billion, while actual Q3 2026 revenue was $16.956 billion — quarter-on-quarter growth down from 144% in Q2 to 47%, back to the level of Q4 2025, just before Anthropic's coding business took off.

The filing also still does not update the October ARR figure the market cares about, a number that may directly affect the listing price.

We discussed, principally:

- Markets are swinging hard in the short term. Is this the signal that the AI bubble is bursting?
- Taking apart the prospectus: the quality of Anthropic's revenue, and its compute spending.
- On the demand side, what comes after coding?
- As the goal shifts from the strongest model to usable value and return on investment, what new opportunities are opening up?

Lu Zhang founded Fusion Fund in 2015, investing mainly in US B2B technology, with holdings including SpaceX, and a focus on AI's intersection with healthcare, financial services and space technology. Before founding Amont Partners in 2023, Rob worked at Morgan Stanley, Slate Path Capital and elsewhere.

Two investors from different backgrounds share one judgement: the root of the problem is on the demand side. How much will enterprises keep spending on AI, and how fast? One sees a crisis; the other sees opportunity.

## Is this the eve of the bubble bursting?

**LatePost**: Having read Anthropic's prospectus, what makes you more positive on the company?

**Lu Zhang**: What struck me most is that Anthropic already has nearly 12,000 enterprise customers spending over $100,000 a year, more than 1,900 of them spending over $1 million — quite unlike the early state of excessive customer concentration. It shows Claude has been integrated fairly deeply into enterprise workflows, gradually moving from a tool to infrastructure.

**Rob**: The best piece of news is that Anthropic's customer concentration is lower than I expected. In the first nine months of this year the top five customers' share of revenue fell to about 25%, and the largest single customer accounts for under 10%. I'd expect that to keep falling.

**LatePost**: What makes you more cautious?

**Lu Zhang**: Mainly the relationship between revenue growth and capital investment. Anthropic has a lot of cash and strong access to funding, but its compute spending is also very large. So I'll keep watching its actual operating margin, cash burn, and expected future spending on training and compute.

**Rob**: Anthropic claims operating cash flow has turned positive, but it played a small trick: it signed over $160 billion in compute finance-lease agreements with Broadcom, turning operating cash flow into financing cash flow to make free cash flow look better. (Note: under US GAAP, the principal portion of finance lease payments is recorded in financing cash flow, and interest usually in operating cash flow. Although the Anthropic-Broadcom contract is a lease in instalments, as of September 2026 some Broadcom-related compute had come online and Anthropic had begun recognising lease costs and paying rent.)

**LatePost**: One thing the market is watching closely is Anthropic's valuation when it actually lists, which will certainly have a short-term effect on public markets. The initial consensus was $2 trillion; in mid-October there were reports it might be cut to $1.5 trillion. Where do you think it lands?

**Lu Zhang**: The discussion now leans toward $1.5 trillion, and I think landing at $1.5 trillion rather than higher is actually healthier for how it trades afterwards.

Anthropic has also raised a great deal recently, with both long- and short-horizon investors on the register, and how the lock-up is designed will affect the final price too.

**LatePost**: On lock-ups — the prospectus says non-core employees can sell 10% of their equity on the day of listing. Anthropic now has more than 5,300 people. Could that create meaningful selling pressure?

**Lu Zhang**: It may bring some price volatility. But Anthropic's people have always had a lot of confidence in the company, and its attrition is the lowest among the frontier model companies in Silicon Valley.

**Rob**: I think if they can hold $1.5 trillion, management should be quite satisfied. It depends heavily on a number that hasn't been disclosed: October ARR. (Note: what the market calls ARR appears in Anthropic's S-1 as RRR, Revenue Run-Rate, calculated as usage revenue over the most recent 28 days × 13, plus the most recent month's subscription revenue × 12.)

Anthropic will disclose that figure during the roadshow before listing, and you can then extrapolate linearly from the quarter-on-quarter growth to get year-end ARR.

If year-end ARR can reach $100 billion — the market's expectation back in the summer — then a valuation of $1.5 trillion or above becomes possible. But if the implied year-end figure comes in below the $85–90 billion you'd derive from the first three quarters, then whether it even reaches $1.2–1.3 trillion is a serious question.

**LatePost**: So you personally think $100 billion year-end ARR is hard to hit?

**Rob**: There are many factors. ARR is calculated from the previous month, so it moves with the cycle. In the US market, November and December bring Thanksgiving and Christmas and fewer working days, so growth in the last two months is affected regardless.

**LatePost**: How likely is it that this IPO turns the AI market from boom to bust?

**Lu Zhang**: I don't think the overall market reverses, but the industry really is at an inflection point. For the past few years the most important thing in AI was putting more resources and compute into infrastructure, chasing maximum capability. What matters next is embedding AI into workflows and industries: model capability is no longer first — cost and integration are.

**LatePost**: After the sharp fall in US tech and AI compute stocks, some say we're on the eve of the bubble bursting. Is that a real signal, or panic?

**Rob**: I think it's excessive panic. Whether AI is ultimately vindicated or disproved, whether it's a bubble or not, comes down to downstream demand.

There are in fact positive signals on demand. Amazon, the world's largest cloud provider, gave AWS revenue growth guidance two years out for the first time, saying 2027 and 2028 can both exceed 40%. That is a strong statement of confidence — it means they believe enterprise customers will keep spending on tokens. It's just that the relatively bearish don't believe long-dated guidance, and investors who were already aggressive don't find 40% especially surprising.

Personally, I think it's too early to declare that Amazon is lying.

As for this sell-off, the whole AI sector was under pressure through October and has essentially fallen back to late-July levels. What the market fears most is still demand — revenue growth failing to keep pace with capital expenditure. So heading into earnings season, many hoped the cloud providers would give aggressive revenue guidance to shore up confidence. But Microsoft, Google and Amazon came and went, and none of the Q3 numbers beat expectations.

The companies that fell hardest each had their own reasons. For Amazon it was AWS growth below some investors' expectations. Google's problem is its core business: profit comes mainly from search advertising, and growth there fell from 17% in Q2 to 12%, with the market worried that AI is eating search. Intel underestimated how fast the PC market would decline, and shifting capacity from PCs to server CPUs, where demand is stronger, takes time.

**LatePost**: As an investor with positions in AI infrastructure, you seem quite calm about all this.

**Rob**: That comes from our style. I used to do private equity, where you have to be prepared to hold anything for ten years or more, so I don't worry much about short-term swings in sentiment.

And our strategy is fairly Buffett-like: no leverage. Micron, for instance — we started buying around $50 in 2022, and it peaked above $1,200 this year, with at least three or four drawdowns of more than 30% along the way.

With leverage you do have to watch short-term trading sentiment, because even if the logic is right you can die on the way there — Situational Awareness blew up in July. I'm not saying AI will definitely work out; just that it's too early to call.

**LatePost**: Your main concern is the durability of demand. On the other side, plenty of people are discussing risk on the compute supply side — companies issuing debt to fund compute, borrowing more, at higher cost. Google, Amazon and other giants have had quarters of negative free cash flow this year. If demand really disappoints, what happens on the supply side?

**Rob**: It depends whether there's a moat. Many NeoClouds have no real moat; they're pure commodity businesses. CoreWeave and Nebius have some software capability and so some moat. A lot of the rest — including some that recently tried and failed to list, like Nscale — are essentially real estate businesses, because all they're selling is land and long-term power purchase agreements, which has nothing to do with technology. Those could be very risky from here.

Industries with real moats are different. Memory, for example: only three companies in the world can make it, and the supply-demand gap is widening year by year. Those companies have good cash flow and don't need to borrow. For us as investors, that risk-reward is far better.

**LatePost**: If a chain reaction of falling compute demand started with the NeoClouds, could it become a debt crisis on the scale of 2008?

**Rob**: I don't think the hit to the US economy would be as large as 2008–09. First, all AI-related investment together is under 3% of US GDP; before the subprime crisis, real estate was a far larger share.

Second, back then the US banking system was the main lender, whereas most of the money going into AI today doesn't pass through banks — it comes from private credit, various funds, and equity markets. So even if things blow up, the impact on banks is relatively contained. As long as the banking system holds, it's hard for the US economy to end up where it was in 2008–09.

**LatePost**: If you picked two or three metrics to track after Anthropic lists, what would they be?

**Lu Zhang**: First, enterprise customer retention and future expansion, which determine whether revenue can keep growing and outrun spending.

Second, Anthropic's core product is coding, but more and more non-coding use cases are starting to contribute revenue. I want to see whether it can extend coding successfully into broader enterprise workflows and cover more applications.

**Rob**: First, the customer mix I mentioned, which shows whether it's over-dependent on a few large accounts.

Second, customer expansion, which shows whether Anthropic keeps delivering value. That corresponds to NRR (net revenue retention) in the S-1, which is very high right now — 900% in Q3 year on year. But if the base is very low, a high NRR doesn't mean much. Anthropic's revenue began surging late last year into early this year, so watch what the number does from here.

Third, revenue per unit of compute, which reflects earning power. That's been improving: two years ago every $1 of compute spending corresponded to less than $1 of revenue; now it's over $2. If that keeps rising, the business model works.

## Closed-model usage is falling, and no single player will take the market

**LatePost**: Anthropic chose to list within 2026 while OpenAI pushed its listing to 2027. How does the order matter?

**Lu Zhang**: Even if OpenAI wanted to list this year, its internal readiness and financials might not meet the bar. And Anthropic's growth is already slowing, so listing while market enthusiasm is high is the better choice.

Also, the capital available to support trillion-dollar companies is finite, so Anthropic listing first affects where money flows and will certainly create more uncertainty for OpenAI in the near term.

**LatePost**: Many VCs have invested in Anthropic, yet the body of the prospectus repeatedly mentions Spark Capital, and its partner Yasmin Razavi is the only VC representative on the board. What role has that firm played?

**Lu Zhang**: Spark was founded in 2005 and has backed Coinbase, Twitter and others. It invested in Anthropic in May 2023, not long after ChatGPT set off the global wave, when there was no consensus that another frontier model company could emerge. Yasmin also pushed Anthropic strategically toward making coding its focus.

That was a crucial choice for Anthropic. A definition of AGI I like is: "AI can do 90% of human work, and do it better than 90% of people." Among all agents today, the only one that meets that standard is coding.

That said, Spark's frequency in the S-1 also reflects the fact that its partner is a director and more compliance disclosure is required. You can't infer a firm's influence simply from how often it appears.

**LatePost**: Anthropic has never invested heavily in consumer. When consumer growth was strong, some called that a weakness; when coding and enterprise did well, people called it strategic focus. Long term, how do you see focusing on enterprise and doing fewer things?

**Lu Zhang**: I'm biased answering this, because for more than a decade I've invested only in B2B, never consumer. I personally think it's the right strategy.

First, Anthropic isn't the first large-model company, it's the second, so it should concentrate limited resources on finding differentiation. OpenAI's focus then was consumer users; Anthropic saw enterprise demand. That also suits it, because this is a company that emphasised safety from the day it was founded, and enterprise customers value that highly.

At the same time, when resources are limited, consumer feedback isn't the most efficient way to iterate AI: the needs are more diffuse, the feedback less structured, and consumers' willingness and ability to pay are lower than enterprises'.

I don't think Anthropic needs to compete with OpenAI in every market. This market is large and varied enough that it won't hold only one company.

**LatePost**: OpenAI and Anthropic also both face competition from open models. What's changing there?

**Lu Zhang**: More large enterprise customers really are reducing their share of closed-model usage. AT&T recently said publicly that over the next few years it wants 60–70% of employees' AI requests handled by open models, up from 40% now.

But I don't think open models rising necessarily hurts closed ones; what matters is how big the whole market is. The entire economy is integrating AI now. The tech industry is under 10% of US GDP; what comes next is integrating industries that make up 30%, 50% and more. As I said, the future market won't be dominated by one or two model companies.

## Reading the prospectus closely

### Growth is slowing: Q3 2026 is back to where Q4 2025 was, before coding took off

**LatePost**: The prospectus discloses Q2 revenue of $11.555 billion; combined with earlier reports of $16.8 billion in Q3, quarter-on-quarter growth has fallen to 45%, back to Q4 2025 levels, before coding took off. How do you read that drop? (After this interview, Anthropic updated the S-1 with Q3 revenue of $16.956 billion.)

**Rob**: 45% quarter-on-quarter is far below market expectations. There could be many factors. Competition, for one: OpenAI shipped the very strong GPT-6 series in Q3. There's also what the S-1 disclosed — 5% to 7% of Anthropic's revenue had been coming from competitors' "distillation," and they banned those accounts, which also hit growth. Though that's one-off and unrelated to the core business.

So public markets are now watching the October ARR I mentioned, which is the key to pricing and to short-term sentiment.

**LatePost**: The prospectus says more than half of the Fortune 500 are already Anthropic customers. Does that mean it has penetrated large accounts substantially?

**Rob**: What it discloses is that more than half of the Fortune 500 spent at least $100,000 in a year — not how much they actually spent, whether just over $100,000, or $1 million, or $10 million. If the average per customer isn't that high, it actually implies revenue growth ahead could be fast, and that there's plenty of room to penetrate further.

**LatePost**: On customer expansion, Anthropic's NRR is 900%, where an excellent SaaS company is around 120–130%. If it fell back to 200–300%, would public markets treat that as a blow-up?

**Rob**: It depends how fast it falls. If it drops to the range of an ordinary mature company within a single year, that would obviously hit sentiment.

**LatePost**: Another question touching both revenue and cost is the revenue share paid to cloud providers. The prospectus doesn't disclose it directly; derived from revenue and cost it's about 37% — higher than the 30% "Apple tax" mobile apps pay the App Store. How do you read that?

**Rob**: Right now that money is worth spending. Anthropic's sales team is small, and the only way to support sales at this scale is to rely on Amazon and the other clouds. And the clouds are not just a channel: they are investors — Amazon has put a great deal into Anthropic — as well as compute providers, and they can offer structured lease financing when Anthropic buys compute.

**LatePost**: Anthropic is also carrying receivables from the clouds: in the first half of this year, cloud-marketplace-related receivables were 58% of net receivables, and receivables were 35.8% of first-half revenue. Why this structure? Will it persist?

**Rob**: The clouds are in a very strong position right now. Many enterprise relationships don't sit with Anthropic or OpenAI but with the clouds and with data warehouse companies like Snowflake and Databricks. For an enterprise customer, bypassing them to buy tokens directly from Anthropic isn't attractive:

One, the token price through these companies is essentially the same as going to Anthropic directly.

Two, enterprise customers have worked with Amazon and Snowflake for years, and when integration work is needed those firms can send people; Anthropic lacks those teams.

Three, security. Non-tech Fortune 500 companies worry about their data being used to train models, but going through Snowflake and Amazon brings mature security guarantees and clearer accountability if something goes wrong — which suits the CIOs making the decision.

Finally, data companies like Snowflake host the enterprise's data and understand its business better, so they have an efficiency advantage: facing the same internal problem with the same model, a data company may be able to optimise and solve it while burning fewer tokens.

**LatePost**: Anthropic and OpenAI have also set up joint ventures with large PE firms this year and built FDE (forward-deployed engineer) teams, so clearly they want to serve enterprise customers directly. How will that change the division of labour and the competition?

**Rob**: They'll certainly do it — they want to hold the customer relationship themselves. But it's a business that depends enormously on people, pushed customer by customer with sales and service staff, and it takes a long time to build.

**LatePost**: When a company is already in a faster-growing business, it may not adapt well to a slower one.

**Rob**: Right. Google has been in cloud for years, and its service capability with enterprise customers still lags Amazon and Microsoft, who have been at it longer.

### The structure of compute spending: paying long leases, collecting short ones

**LatePost**: Per the updated prospectus, Anthropic's committed long-term compute contracts total $895.4 billion. How do you read that scale and the risk in it?

**Rob**: The headline number alone doesn't mean much; what matters is whether the money must be paid. The maximum contract value disclosed with SpaceX, for instance, is $120.5 billion, but the irrevocable period ends in Q1 2027, after which either side can cancel with 90 days' notice. Contracts with smaller clouds, such as Nscale, can also be left unperformed in specified circumstances.

Other deals are rigid and irrevocable — most typically the $161.2 billion agreement with Broadcom, which not only cannot be cancelled but requires Anthropic to provide a parent guarantee and equipment collateral.

**LatePost**: There's also a maturity mismatch: many of Anthropic's compute contracts are long-term and irrevocable, and some data centre power and gas contracts run twenty years, during which Anthropic also bears price increases in gas and pipeline facilities. On the revenue side, its own customers have no long-term contractual commitment to it. What might that lead to?

**Rob**: Fundamentally this is still a demand question.

There are two curves. One is the rise in compute spending, which is relatively clear: memory prices, for instance, certainly aren't coming down in the next few years, because expanding capacity takes a long time and annual growth over the coming years is only 20-odd percent — including the higher-end capacity CXMT and YMTC in China are working on.

The other is the AI revenue curve, and that's the big variable. For AI revenue to catch up with compute spending, for the two curves to cross, existing customers have to actually see ROI and be willing to spend more, and new customers have to be won.

## What comes after coding?

**LatePost**: NRR tracks customer expansion — whether enterprises keep spending — but it's a lagging indicator. Is there a leading indicator for changes in demand?

**Rob**: We haven't seen a good leading indicator yet. Ask many companies' CIOs how much they'll spend next year or the year after and they don't know themselves. How much money, headcount and hours the tokens they burn actually save, and how much revenue they add — they haven't worked out how to calculate it either.

There is a coincident indicator, though: revenue at the clouds and at data companies like Databricks and Snowflake, because most of the Fortune 500 burn tokens through them. They can see in near real time what enterprise customers are doing day to day: who burns a lot, who burns little, how it's changing. Amazon's guidance for next year and the year after, which we've mentioned several times, is an example.

**LatePost**: I know Fusion Fund has a network of large-enterprise CIOs and CTOs. What have you observed?

**Lu Zhang**: In summary, AI enters an enterprise in two phases. The first is business automation — folding AI into existing workflows, mainly to cut cost and raise efficiency. The second is business optimisation, aimed at increasing revenue. Different industries and departments are at different phases now, and see different ROI.

From our own observation — we built the Fusion CXO network back in 2018, and it now brings together more than forty CTOs and chief AI officers from Fortune 500 companies. First, since 2026 these companies have markedly raised the priority of AI and data strategy; many created a chief AI officer in the past six months. We now convene more often than the quarterly cadence we used to keep, and they increasingly invite us to present on AI at their board meetings.

Not long ago, for instance, we published a pharmaceutical industry survey, having spoken with most of the world's top 20 drugmakers; 60–70% of them put AI at the top of their strategic priorities. This year Eli Lilly and Nvidia, and Merck and Google, both announced sizeable partnerships.

I also know of a well-known US bank whose AI budget this year has reached over $10 billion.

That said, we should have realistic expectations about how long Fortune 500 adoption takes, because most enterprises neither want nor are able to change their workflows immediately. As I said, AI enters an enterprise in two steps; the first step may take three to five years, and the second longer still.

Because a lot has to be solved along the way. Transparency and security, for instance — how many people are using how many AI tools, and which core data do those tools touch? And cost: many companies are moving to open models precisely because closed models are too expensive.

**LatePost**: You mentioned large non-tech enterprises already have substantial AI budgets. Is that because they're genuinely seeing returns in practice, or because they've moved into larger-scale experimentation?

**Lu Zhang**: It's fair to say they're still experimenting, but with a much stronger consensus about AI.

What they're thinking now isn't "will some startup disrupt me," but "if my competitor uses AI and I don't, my market share gets eaten." Because in theory AI lets a small, high-quality team create far more value.

By the same logic, where a market's 80% share might once have been split among 30 companies, in future it may be only five to eight, because they have rebuilt their products and customer service with AI. So you find that once one large company in an industry starts using AI, the others quickly follow — they're all afraid of being second to move.

There are also many enormous private companies in the US. I've spoken with some large American family businesses that put billions of dollars into AI, automation and physical AI years ago. They have no public-company earnings pressure, smaller boards and thick cash flow, so they can be more flexible in investing in AI.

**LatePost**: Anthropic's and the large models' main revenue growth comes from coding. What's the next use case that could match coding's scale and speed?

**Rob**: I think it's replacing white-collar knowledge work. The coding market itself is fairly small — there are only a few million programmers in the US and penetration is already high — while replacing blue-collar work with robots involves the complexity of the physical world and will scale far more slowly than software.

**Lu Zhang**: There may not be another single product as dominant as coding. Coding has several characteristics. One, the tech industry adopts new technology faster. Two, it's high-frequency, high-value per task, and results are verified quickly. Three, it completes the task directly rather than offering advice or assistance. Whereas the legal and financial work people discuss so widely isn't a setting that produces a finished result directly. We shouldn't expect every use case to take off the way coding did.

As for Anthropic specifically, I think it should now strengthen its overall enterprise platform — not merely supplying models but becoming the main infrastructure for enterprise agent workflows. Claude Cowork, for instance, connecting software through MCP to create a middle-platform effect. Back-office workflows have a degree of commonality across companies: finance, compliance, operations, supply chain, inventory, sales. If the entry point for all of those workflows is Cowork, that matters enormously to Anthropic.

In terms of industry demand and scale, I'm personally positive on healthcare. Healthcare is nearly 20% of US GDP, with large volumes of high-quality data and varied use cases, some of them with highly standardised workflows. If data sensitivity and regulation can be handled, AI will move quickly in healthcare.

**LatePost**: There's a view that as AI spreads beyond coding into slower, more fragmented use cases, the opportunity tilts toward startups — because for Anthropic and OpenAI, now giants in practice, the growth rhythm of those use cases doesn't match the coding battlefield they're already invested in.

**Lu Zhang**: Yes, this is a good year for startups. It's the inflection point I mentioned at the start: people are no longer chasing model capability alone but are focused, more pragmatically, on how AI spreads through industries.

At the application layer, vertical applications in highly regulated industries are an opening for startups. One company we've invested in is building a vertical model for cell therapy, and recently reached a Parkinson's partnership with Novo Nordisk.

OpenAI and Anthropic of course want these markets too, but do they really want to fight startups on vertical models and use-case-specific customisation? That isn't necessarily their optimal strategy.

Many drugmakers and insurers are also more willing to share data with startups than to hand it to Anthropic or OpenAI. Anthropic recently started operating a wet lab, which looks like an attempt to work on drugs more directly. Pharma companies already using its services will feel that it used their data and is now competing with them. Other industries will worry about the same thing.

This turn will also change the compute layer: the emphasis used to be on building the strongest model with large GPU clusters, whereas since last year into this one, lowering inference cost, heterogeneous compute at inference, and local deployment are all creating new openings for startups.

**LatePost**: Finally, what important question are you thinking about where the answer isn't yet clear?

**Rob**: Still the demand question I raised. The spending curve is now quite clear; the slope of the demand and revenue curve isn't.

Whether the so-called bubble bursts comes down to whether equity or debt markets can hold out until the moment demand catches up with compute spending.

**Lu Zhang**: I've been thinking a lot about AI regulation. People used to think regulation could wait — deploy the product first. But now, if AI is to go deeper into enterprise processes, and especially with some companies considering deploying physical AI, safety becomes very pressing. CTOs at some industry leaders are anxious about this: they need regulatory policy factored in before they can deploy physical AI at scale.

But AI regulation is extremely complex. It has to account for where the technology is heading, and it involves cross-regional and cross-industry coordination; and whether it ends up regulated industry by industry or under unified cross-industry standards is still unanswered. That's both an opportunity and a challenge, and I'd like to see more founders, executives and investors pay attention to it.
