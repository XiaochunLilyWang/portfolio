*Passing Guanghua Tower / bracing with all my strength against the demon wind / and off I fly*

*Morning alarm sounds / I drag myself up to scan in for exercise / the mini-program crashes*

…

The haiku, from Japan, is finding new life on Chinese social platforms.

These seemingly casual three-line verses, in their five-seven-five syllable form, have become a new vehicle for young people's feelings. From griping about daily life to commenting on current affairs, to simply forcing a single sentence into three lines — these three short lines seem to hold everything, filled with the whole range of living.

How did we come to know the haiku, and why have we started writing them? In a *Fudan Weekly* survey, some had read about the form in a textbook, and others came to it through an interest in Japanese literature; but more students knew it from the recent vogue for writing haiku online. "For laughs," "to record my life," "trading verses with friends," "entering a contest on a social platform" — the reasons students write them are more varied still.

The haiku long ago passed beyond being a symbol in old texts and has gradually become a cultural practice anyone can join. Its history, past and present, may offer a deeper gloss on this phenomenon.

## A few hundred years back: where the haiku came from, and how it was written

Zou Bo, associate professor in the Japanese department of Fudan's College of Foreign Languages and Literatures, explained to *Fudan Weekly* that before the haiku, the more popular form of poetry in Japan was the waka, dominated by the tanka in a five-seven-five-seven-seven syllable pattern. Originating in Japan's Nara period, it was the core form of expression in the aristocratic culture of the time. In the late Heian period a form derived from waka appeared: renga. It began as a game in which two people composed a waka together — a linked-verse poem written collaboratively — structured into a hokku (the opening verse, five-seven-five) and a hiraku (seven-seven). Renga was elegant and classical in tone, inheriting a Chinese aesthetic sensibility. In time renga was gradually displaced by a humorous poetry called haikai.

Haikai shared renga's form but used more wordplay and puns, giving it a witty, comic quality. As haikai developed, people began publishing the hokku as an independent work, and it eventually settled into the haiku, built on a metre of three lines and seventeen syllables in a five-seven-five pattern. A haiku also needs a kigo, a seasonal word, and a kireji, a cutting word marking a pause and leaving space.

The Edo period was the haiku's peak, and its most famous poet is Matsuo Bashō, the "saint of haiku." He gave the form a higher artistry, turning it from a witty little ditty into a short poem capable of carrying feeling. Beside Japan's famous rapids, the Mogami River, he wrote "五月雨を／あつめて早し／最上川" — roughly, "Gathering the rains / of the fifth month, swift and full / the Mogami River" — which has endured as a classic.

By the mid-Edo period, as literacy spread among ordinary people, the haiku produced a more popular variant: senryū. The presence or absence of a seasonal word is the key difference between the two. The form was shaped by the tenja — the professional judge of renga and haikai in Edo — Karai Senryū, who gathered outstanding pieces into the *Haifū Yanagidaru* selections, which were widely admired. His student Goryōken Arubeshi then selected the briefest, liveliest and clearest of these, named them after his predecessor Karai Senryū, and published the collection, spreading the form widely.

Senryū's relative formal freedom brought variety in subject matter: anything from the sweep of history to the small change of daily life can be put into it. Where the haiku pursues elegance, the senryū prizes wit or comic flair, and in emotional register it resembles China's doggerel verse — well suited to expressing feeling directly. Because haiku and senryū are formally identical, some works do not distinguish between them strictly.

## Centuries old: how it reached the internet, where everyone writes

Because the haiku is such a representative form in Japanese poetry, it appears in many Japanese or Japan-related ACGN works (animation, comics, games and novels). The wide circulation of those works online gradually broke the haiku out of its niche, from a subculture popular among a few enthusiasts into an internet meme anyone could join — fragments of nonsense symbolism in the online world.

In the 2011 anime *Nichijou*, the protagonist Aioi Yuuko, made to stand in the corridor for not doing her homework, composes a haiku out of boredom: "Forgot my homework / and so I stand in the hall / the Mogami River." The verse spoofs Bashō's Mogami River poem above. But "Mogami River" is used here only to fill out the syllables and carries no real meaning.

The haiku shows up in plenty of other ACGN works too. The anime *Senryu Girl* tells a youthful love story in which Nanako Yukishiro and Eiji Busujima are brought together by senryū; the film *Words Bubble Up Like Soda Pop* summarised its plot in promotional material with a haiku: "Seventeen years passed / and then, in the height of July / at last I met you." And in the Chinese game *Genshin Impact*, the character Kaedehara Kazuha is written as fond of composing verse, and recites in his character trailer: "High sky, scattered cloud / sunset clears to my homeland's shape / a wanderer's drifting heart." Works like these brought the haiku into ordinary households, and its specialist, serious character gradually dissolved.

The recent wave of haiku writing on Chinese social platforms began with a video posted on 19 December 2024 by the Bilibili creator MOJi辞書, explaining Japan's national haiku contest. The video focused on the winning entry — a moving haiku by a high school senior recalling watching fireflies with her mother, who had cancer. In the comments, the creator launched a Chinese-language haiku writing activity, prompting a great many attempts. The five-seven-five form is easy to write and pleasant to read aloud, and "Chinese-style haiku" quickly became a meme alive in comment sections across social platforms.

Zou Bo points out that whereas a single character in a Japanese haiku may take several syllables to pronounce, the one-character-one-syllable nature of Chinese means a single character can be understood as the smallest unit of language, and deconstructed and recombined far more flexibly.

"Mogamigawa" is a proper noun, a river in Japan. But in Chinese, the character 最 (*zui*, "most") is commonly used as an adjective or adverb. So enthusiasts of Chinese-style haiku broke 最 out as an independent meaningful unit. People now often play the straight man beneath someone else's haiku in the format "most XX."

This collective composition has split into two forms. One is commenting on an event through the haiku form. A user posting a question about the haiku craze, for instance, can collect endless answers in haiku form in the comments.

The other is free, imaginative composition on a given theme. Plenty of users have launched "haiku contests," where the poster sets a topic and invites others to write around it in five-seven-five.

On "most delicious," one user wrote "Spicy chicken burger / crisp chicken wings and hash browns / grilled chicken burger," making no secret of a love of fast food. Under "most Shanghai," the comments produced "Off to take Line 4 / board the purple train with confidence / it's Line 3," mocking the predicament of getting the wrong one of Shanghai's partly shared Lines 3 and 4. In the discussion of "most university": "There's an eight a.m. class / turn off the alarm, flop back down / open my eyes at ten," a resigned account of students who cannot wake up. Terse haiku, comments batted back and forth — these few humorous, sharp lines give life a new gloss.

A literary form with a fixed count and shape naturally brings to mind filling in the tune-patterns of Song ci poetry. Yet compared with ci, a treasure of Chinese culture settled over a thousand years, the haiku's history runs only a few hundred, and its works circulate nowhere near as widely as the classics of Su Shi or Li Qingzhao. Both are fixed forms — so why is it the haiku that has set off a wave of mass composition on today's social platforms?

By convention, writing poetry in Chinese comes with requirements of tone, metre and rhyme — dancing in shackles, which puts people off. The "Chinese-style haiku" popular today is a de-ruled reconstruction of a foreign form, free of the Japanese constraints of kigo and kireji, grafted instead onto the meme culture of Chinese social media; the barrier to writing is flattened. On length alone, even a short ci runs to 30 or 40 characters, demanding a great deal in word choice and testing the reader's patience. A haiku of only seventeen characters fits the fragmented way information travels on social media.

Zou Bo also believes the haiku's popularity owes something to an aesthetic habit formed over centuries. From the four-character lines of the *Book of Songs*, to the five- and seven-character lines of Tang poetry, to the varied lines of Song ci, we grow up with poetry and are highly sensitive to the rhythm and cadence of language; the haiku is not an unfamiliar form to us. Historically, the haiku and its predecessor the waka were themselves products of Chinese poetry evolving after it reached Japan. China and Japan, "different lands, the same wind and moon," share an aesthetic feeling for natural imagery. Bashō's line "夏草や兵どもが夢の跡" — summer grasses, all that remains of warriors' dreams — was inspired by Du Fu's "The state is shattered, the mountains and rivers remain; spring in the city, grass and trees grow deep." And now the haiku is feeding Chinese internet users' own writing in turn.

## So popular here: does the West write them too?

The haiku's popularity on the Chinese internet owes much to the short, fast rhythm of social media and to the shared roots of Chinese and Japanese culture. So can the haiku leave the Chinese-character cultural sphere and travel from East Asia to the West?

In fact the haiku reached Western countries before the First World War. Many have a literature written in their own language in imitation of the form. In the English-speaking world, the haiku's development passed roughly through three phases: Imagist haiku, the haiku of the Beat Generation, and the collective composition of the contemporary internet.

The first person to write poetry in English approximating the haiku was the American writer Paul Reps (1895–1990). Born in the United States, he studied art and literature, travelled widely, lived in Japan, and was deeply influenced by the haiku and by Zen philosophy. He began writing poetry in 1939 and is considered one of America's earliest haiku poets, his work fusing Eastern philosophy with Western modernist technique. In "Snow man / snow woman / melting away in the sun," for example, "snow" can be read as the haiku's seasonal word. Working from the sound patterns of English itself, the English-language haiku began to cultivate its own aesthetic while still emphasising the classical Japanese principles.

In the early twentieth century, Imagism rose in Anglo-American poetry, and a group of poets led by the American Ezra Pound (1885–1972) argued for learning from the East, bringing the theory of the image from Chinese poetry and the Japanese haiku into English verse. Pound's best-known poem, "In a Station of the Metro" — "The apparition of these faces in the crowd, / Petals on a wet, black bough" — compresses a set of images, freezing a moment in the metro station like a frame pulled from a film, and accords entirely with the classical haiku's terseness and long aftertaste: the words end, the meaning does not.

In this period the haiku was still being introduced and translated, and a considerable part of these poets' work was later called "like-haiku." But the form gradually combined with original composition in English, laying the ground for the English haiku that took shape among the Beat Generation, the American literary movement that began after the Second World War.

In that period's practice, the English haiku escaped the Japanese mould, dropped the constraint of the seasonal word, and became formally freer. Through those poets' efforts, the English haiku became organically part of American culture. Jack Kerouac (1922–1969), one of the Beats' central figures, put his view of writing haiku in English this way:

Then I'll invent
The American Haiku type:
The simple rhyming triolet:
——Seventeen syllables?
No, as I say, American Pops:
——Simple 3 line poems.

Kerouac held that the American haiku should not be bound to five-seven-five: anything in three lines should count. He named the American form "pop," to mark it off from "haiku." Compared with the Japanese haiku's attention to nature, his work turns more toward people. In "Evening coming / The office girl / unloosing her scarf," a moment of a working woman adjusting her clothes at the end of the day becomes the occasion for a poem.

After the millennium, the haiku had a vogue on the Western internet too. In 2001 the American writer Nanette Wylde launched the "haikU" project, where users could submit their own haiku to a database and also generate one at random from it, producing work that was "coherently poetic without a single author." The project used technology to drive mass co-creation of haiku and drew participants worldwide. And on social media abroad today — Reddit, X — the haiku challenge tag endures.

Zou Bo notes that translating haiku has long been a vexed problem in cross-cultural transmission. Whether a haiku can travel widely in another language depends significantly on whether the target language's form corresponds. In Chinese translation, because Chinese characters are one character to one syllable while Japanese characters may take several, a Japanese syllable carries less information than a Chinese one — 大学, "university," is *daigaku*, four syllables in Japanese and plainly two in Chinese. Translated, fewer Chinese characters suffice to say the same thing, so a haiku can also be rendered in a three-four-three pattern, or in two traditional five-character lines.

The same holds for other languages: complete formal equivalence is very hard, and the five-seven-five form usually becomes a variant in translation. Zou Bo notes two exceptions among Western languages, Italian and Turkish, whose sound patterns are relatively close to Japanese and which can likewise end on vowels, so that in theory they can translate and compose in five-seven-five more faithfully.

Historically, the haiku's globalisation has two layers. The first is global reading. With its compressed form and universal aesthetic appeal, the haiku has already crossed cultural borders to become a conspicuous coordinate on the map of world literature. The second, deeper layer is global composition. Imitations of the haiku are not scarce in the literatures of various countries, but genuine cases of creative cross-cultural transformation remain rare. This difficulty at the level of composition reflects fundamental differences between the prosodic systems of different languages. To make the global leap from being read to being written, the haiku may need more local innovations of the kind Chinese social media's "most XX" represents.

## Everyone's writing them: it's only seventeen characters, so I'll try too

As the contemporary Japanese poet Takahashi Mutsuo has said, the haiku arrived as a form of asobi — play. As social life grows more atomised, the haiku is like a firefly's small light piercing loneliness. The carnival of haiku writing in social media comment sections is at times a dissonant echo of Dazai Osamu's *No Longer Human* in the Chinese-speaking world. Young souls catch sight of their own fragments in the confession of being born human, and build floating bridges out of humour and self-mockery, leading somewhere further off. Through words we record our lives, let out the self within, work off what sits heavy in the chest, and answer the world with a song.

Before March runs out
I've spent far too much shopping
Plain noodles next month
——Chichichi

春雨や／傘捨て泥避／軒佇み
Spring rain, fine and cold
I won't walk my shoes through mud
Waiting out the sky
——lj

A haiku contest
but all I can write is senryū
I withdraw, regretful
——Chiba

Through the seasons and the three meals of daily life, the haiku still carries the Zen of Bashō gazing at an old pond. We turn the moving shadows of a campus spring into seasonal words, write the rice and salt of daily life into a few lines of verse — and these cross-sections of language, caught and written in an instant, are themselves a kind of cutting word, a pause in the poem. Today's haiku is a window for observing the world and letting feeling out. Those small slips of haiku scattered through time were, all along, the fleeting gifts of living.
