Social media has become a new window through which some bank employees buy their way to performance targets, paying above-market interest to attract deposits. Money brokers have made the internet their base of operations, moving between banks and depositors. They open shops on e-commerce platforms whose main business is "acting as an agent for banking services," and some bank employees buy those services online to hit their quotas. A grey industry chain built around banks' internal targets is gradually taking shape.

## From month-end deposits to pension accounts: bank targets go shopping online

Search terms like "hitting bank targets" or "pulling in deposits" on Xiaohongshu, Douyin or Weibo, and you will find a great many bank employees venting: some lament that their assessment quotas are impossible to meet, others ask strangers online to deposit money with them, promising to pay interest in return.

"Wan-10, 500,000 yuan, message me if you're interested!" Posts from bank employees asking for help routinely draw comments like this one. "Wan-10" is industry shorthand: for every 10,000 yuan deposited, the depositor receives 10 yuan in interest per day — so 500,000 yuan earns 500 yuan a day.

The users leaving these comments include depositors with cash on hand, and no shortage of money brokers who trade in deposits for a living. This reporter sent a direct message to one such user on Xiaohongshu, who said he "has personal funds and partners up as an intermediary." At the end of the second quarter, he said, his team was trading deposits at a volume of "roughly 500 million yuan per person."

Mr. Zhang, a money broker, explained how it works: branch managers at some banks quote him a price and hand him the interest subsidy, and he then passes the bank's requirement on to depositors. The depositor puts the money into an account in their own name, withdraws it after the agreed period, and collects the subsidy. "It's very safe — the money just spends a night in your own card." Between July 1 and midday on July 6, he had already transferred more than 10,000 yuan in subsidies to depositors via WeChat, ranging from 500 to 3,000 yuan each.

Mr. Li, another broker, told this reporter that the "market rate" for the subsidy climbed above "wan-20" at the end of the second quarter, and sits between "wan-6" and "wan-7" at the close of other months. Bank employees who want to work with him pay an additional handling fee of around "wan-1."

Deposits are not the only target being bought. Bank employees said that personal pension account openings and digital yuan registrations have also moved online. "One township branch, all elderly customers, and we're told to register ten digital yuan accounts a day." "Suddenly landed with 100 pension account quotas." Money brokers have found a new line of business. On Taobao, JD.com and Pinduoduo, listings and shops offering to "complete bank tasks on your behalf" have appeared and multiplied; some shops report monthly sales above 50,000, with thousands of product reviews.

Asking customer service at a number of Taobao shops, this reporter found that the figures on most product pages are merely nominal. Sellers quote by category, according to the difficulty and turnaround of the banking task and the type and location of the bank. A tier-four digital yuan account costs under 10 yuan each; a tier-two account runs 20 to 30 yuan. Tier-three services range from tens of yuan to more than a hundred. Some shops have filled more than three thousand orders — and keep raising their prices.

Xu Mingdong, deputy dean of the Institute of Finance at Fudan University's School of Economics, said that chasing targets through money brokers and offline canvassing has been an open secret in the banking industry for years. The recent rise of employees "shopping for performance" online, he said, is another expression of deposit-gathering quotas being pushed down onto individual staff.

## The one-day deposit: risk behind an arrangement that seems to suit everyone

Experts point out that compared with the old model of offline canvassing, moving deposit solicitation and account registration onto social platforms does more than raise banks' costs and fuel destructive competition that disrupts the development of the financial industry — it also creates a risk of personal data leaking.

Ji Ligang, executive dean of Fudan University's Institute of Chinese Financial Rule of Law, said banking services are "public" in character: a loss of liquidity can easily destabilise finance and the wider economy, which is why banks' business conduct and the professional qualifications of their staff are subject to "strong regulation" under law and industry self-discipline. Whether it is asking strangers for help on social media, buying promotional services on an e-commerce platform, or taking deposits through money brokers, he said, these various online methods of hitting targets are all illegal or in breach of regulations.

Article 12 of the E-Commerce Law of the People's Republic of China provides that "where an e-commerce operator engages in business activities for which relevant administrative permission is required by law, it shall obtain such administrative permission in accordance with the law." "Analysed through the lens of commercial law, so-called 'completing bank tasks on your behalf' is in substance an e-commerce platform conducting banking business and forming a shadow bank. E-commerce platforms hold no financial licence and cannot conduct banking business," Ji Ligang said.

High-interest deposit solicitation also encourages destructive competition. Ye Xiaohua, senior partner at Beijing Jingsh Law Firm, said: "Through the internet, money brokers mobilise idle funds sitting in depositors' hands, and bring into the open a practice that used to live in a grey zone." In Ji Ligang's view, bank employees subsidising deposits online raises banks' funding costs through the fees paid to brokers, while also disrupting order in the deposit market and constituting unfair competition.

There is also a heightened risk of personal data exposure. The 2021 Notice on Regulating Commercial Banks' Personal Deposit Business Conducted via the Internet states clearly that commercial banks may not conduct fixed-term deposit or flexible deposit business through non-proprietary online platforms, including but not limited to marketing, product display, information transmission, purchase entry points and interest subsidies provided by such platforms. The Measures for the Administration of Consumer Rights Protection by Banking and Insurance Institutions, which took effect this year, require these institutions to establish mechanisms for protecting consumers' personal information, improve internal management systems, tiered approval procedures and internal controls, and apply graded, classified controls across the full lifecycle of consumers' personal information.

Yu Baicheng, chief researcher at Houxue Research, said that customers who are "bought" hand their ID numbers, phone numbers and other details to third parties, making leaks easy. Those third parties lack adequate safeguards for customer information, and some merchants actively leak account details, which can compromise personal data security.

More worrying still, the brokers operate in a grey area. Should a broker collude with a bank employee, there is scope for misappropriation of funds and other unlawful conduct, and for financial loss. Zhao Chunyong, managing partner at Shandong Yiheng Law Firm, put it plainly: the impact on depositors, banks and the financial system is not small — a bank's greatest asset is its reputation, and once problems surface, that reputation falls sharply, destabilising the financial market.

## Cutting the chain will take action on several fronts

Drawn by relatively high returns, a grey industry chain has formed around banks' assorted internal targets. Financial regulators should work with other departments to tighten oversight, and address the roots of the chain.

One cause of practices such as subsidised deposit-taking is that banks' performance assessment systems and metrics are poorly designed, leaving grassroots branches and staff carrying excessive pressure. Banks need to balance business growth against risk control, and short-term gains against long-term interests.

Xu Mingdong argues that using whatever means available to hit targets is speculative behaviour that exposes holes in banks' existing assessment mechanisms. "If banks fully recognised the damage this speculation does to their own interests, they would take measures." For now, however, banks find individual employee conduct difficult to control — and are reluctant to control it — which has allowed some unlawful and non-compliant behaviour to emerge.

Liu Xin, senior partner at Grandall Law Firm (Shanghai), said banks should first strengthen internal compliance and cautionary training, while continuously improving assessment mechanisms and moving away from single, blunt metrics — for example by factoring in account longevity and activity to set scientific, reasonable performance criteria.

Incomplete regulatory mechanisms and insufficient penalties are another important reason these problems persist. In recent years regulators have fined banks over irregular deposit-gathering, but the amounts are generally low, and covert violations are hard to catch in full. Ye Xiaohua argues that regulators must increase penalties for unlawful and non-compliant conduct.

As for social media and e-commerce platforms, when they offer finance-related products and services they should comply with regulators' rules, so as not to encourage bad practice.

"At the moment, faced with a relatively new phenomenon like the publishing of non-compliant financial information, platforms neither fully appreciate the need for control nor have much experience handling it, and so adopt a posture of tacit consent." Liu Xin recommends that social media platforms take effective measures to promptly block, delete or remove financial content identified as non-compliant, so that money brokers cannot use them for unlawful activity; e-commerce platforms, meanwhile, should properly review and vet the services their shops offer, and provide compliance guidance and supervision over how those shops operate.
