Having cataract surgery at the ophthalmology department of Shanghai General Hospital means moving back and forth between two buildings and eleven consulting rooms, from registration to the end of the pre-operative work-up.

Zhou Ping has been here close to ten times. At eight in the morning the hospital is packed, and Zhou Ping is accompanying a 76-year-old through her tests. Booking the appointment, paying, collecting the slips, scanning the code to check in — Zhou Ping handles the paperwork while the old woman simply sits and waits. By the time every pre-operative test is done, it is half past twelve.

After Chinese New Year, Zhou Ping enters his busiest stretch; on his first day back he ran between four hospitals. He is a professional medical companion. Threading through Shanghai's major hospitals, accompanying different patients, playing the part of their "temporary family" — this has been his daily routine for a year.

Today, people calling themselves medical companions take orders online across many platforms, offering to accompany patients to appointments, consult doctors on their behalf, or collect test reports. Companies offering the service are multiplying too: the business information platform Tianyancha lists more than a thousand companion-related enterprises, over 400 of them registered in 2023.

Medical companionship has become a buzzy trade. As demand keeps climbing, more and more people are treating it as a new track for employment or for starting a business. After some time knocking about in the industry, alongside the positive feedback they have also come up against all sorts of constraints and dead ends, and a new worry: where exactly is this industry headed?

## A market of varied needs

In the days after the holiday, Zhou Ping's schedule is full. In Shanghai, Beijing and elsewhere the service is entering a small peak; order volume at Zhou Ping's company is 20% above normal.

Zhou Ping, 40, has been making his way in Shanghai for eight years. He graduated in clinical medicine and worked as a resident physician back home in Henan before coming to Shanghai with the thought of seeing something of the world. For someone at ease with medical basics and with how hospitals work, the job was a natural fit. In January 2023 he joined a companion company full-time.

Liao Yanqin, who has done the work for two years, came to know the industry by chance through her previous job. In 2019, the insurance company she worked for launched an accident policy with medical companionship as an add-on service. As an agent, she decided to do the accompanying herself, to build up medical knowledge and communicate better with clients. The longer she did it, the more she thought about changing careers.

What decided it for both of them was the sheer scale of the need.

By age, the core clientele is the middle-aged and elderly. At the end of 2022 China had 280 million people aged 60 and over, and the population continues to age year by year. "Older people have a strong need to see doctors and collect medication — especially those whose children aren't nearby. They really need someone to go with them." The self-service machines now standard in large hospitals add a further obstacle. On one job, Liao Yanqin watched a doctor get muddled by them too.

The other main group is patients travelling from other parts of the country. Shanghai, rich in medical resources, is a destination for many with serious or hard-to-diagnose illnesses. "These people are seeking treatment far from home, in a place they don't know — you can imagine the difficulties they face in a hospital." Liao Yanqin estimates that of the nearly a thousand clients she has served, more than 60% came from outside the city.

Then there are those having an endoscopy under general anaesthetic, who need a family member present for observation; young people living alone who are afraid to go to hospital by themselves; patients with mental illness who are unwilling to talk to doctors. Patients with widely varying needs go online looking for someone to accompany them, and a market has quietly formed.

## Being a good "temporary family" is not easy

In June 2023, Jin Ming, who works in Guangdong, planned to take his 60-year-old father in Shanghai for cataract surgery. He had assumed it could be done in a day. "There were far too many people. I drove to Shanghai and spent two days with my dad, and all we got through was a pile of pre-operative tests. The surgery was scheduled for twenty-odd days later, and I didn't have time to come back." With both parents unsteady on their feet, Jin Ming was uneasy about letting them go alone, and went online, half-hopefully, to find someone to accompany his father.

In the end a companion named Xiao Juan took his father through the operation and five follow-up visits. Xiao Juan had been a hospital care worker; hearing from a friend that the business was booming, she started posting on Weibo to take orders.

Medical companion is not currently a recognised occupation in China's national occupational classification, and there is no state-issued qualification. Anyone who posts on a personal social media account can call themselves one.

Becoming a companion is not hard, Xiao Juan says; being a good one is. First you have to know the hospital. Having worked as a care worker at several, she has built up a store of hard-won knowledge. "Once I was at Zhongshan Hospital with an elderly man in a wheelchair and had to get him from Building 20 to Building 8. Going via the ground floor means crossing a road — but there's a sky bridge on the second floor, which is far easier."

Each time he "unlocks" a new hospital, Zhou Ping scouts it in advance. On his first cataract job at a top-tier Shanghai hospital he was struck by how punishing it was: "Older people are bound to find it too much — it's exhausting." The patient first sees the attending physician and gets the test orders, takes them to pay, then completes more than a dozen pre-operative tests across eleven consulting rooms. The tests are scattered across different buildings and floors, and each one means queuing at a machine and checking in. "Each test takes under ten minutes, but the waiting is long, and going round that many rooms — even a young person finds it tiring."

Liao Yanqin does a good deal of homework before every job. Once she has the patient's records she looks up the medical terms she doesn't know, sometimes turning to online consultation sites, and walks through the process in her head from the patient's point of view.

At her busiest, Xiao Juan has run five jobs in a single day. On top of a pre-booked proxy consultation, she picked up two medication collections, a biopsy slide collection and a test booking, shuttling between four hospitals. "It rained all day, and I was running non-stop. I was shattered."

## Satisfaction, and helplessness

Companions often deal with the seriously ill, and in those moments their presence can be worth a great deal to someone anxiously awaiting a diagnosis — which in turn gives the companion a sense of accomplishment beyond the job itself.

Liao Yanqin remembers a man in his fifties with a meningioma, who had come from Jiangxi for tests with his son. Before they went into the consulting room, father and son were grim and silent. "They'd been to several hospitals in Jiangxi, and the doctors all said the tumour was benign but needed surgery. They were very worried. The Shanghai doctor took one look at the scan and said it was quite small and already calcified — regular check-ups would do. The moment he finished speaking, the man reached out his hand to me, and I took it without thinking. He just burst into a smile!" In that instant Liao Yanqin was happy for them without meaning to be. "The feeling just carried me along — as if I had become part of their family."

Called "temporary family," companions often do come to treat themselves as relatives. After Jin Ming's father's surgery, Xiao Juan learned that the elderly couple were both unsteady on their feet, and since she happened to live nearby she went to their home twice of her own accord to help put in his eye drops.

"I hope that my being there warms clients at a difficult moment, saves them some running around, and lets them face treatment in better spirits." That is what drew Zhou Ping to the work.

But no one knows whether tomorrow or the unexpected comes first. Late last year Zhou Ping took an emergency job. On the way to the client's home he got a call: the grandmother's condition had suddenly worsened. "I told the client to call an ambulance right away, got off the metro and tried to hail a taxi. It was rush hour, and while I was waiting I got the message that she had died." Zhou Ping was stunned. Beyond offering condolences there was nothing he could do, and he felt utterly powerless.

Being constantly on the move is the normal condition of this work and this life. Companions also face their own problems of career development. Demand is unstable: when Zhou Ping started he had jobs every day, finishing too late for the last metro, but sometimes there are only one or two in a week. There is no standard pricing — the current Shanghai market rate runs from 200 to 600 yuan per four hours. Zhou Ping says his income is very irregular, and nowhere near the "over 10,000 a month" claimed by influencers online. For someone renting alone in Shanghai, it barely covers a living.

As Zhou Ping sees it, most companion companies are micro-enterprises. "A lot of them don't have enough capital or enough clients, and they're not surviving well."

Over two years Liao Yanqin has built a team of 16. In early 2023, believing the industry's prospects were strong, she led them in registering a company; in July its WeChat mini-program went live. Since then, she has found that costs have risen faster than revenue, and that promoting the service is very hard. "The active members of the team can make ten thousand-odd a month," she says. "We can support ourselves, but we can't support the company."

## When does spring arrive?

Since 2023, platforms including Douyin and Xiaohongshu have raised the bar for publishing medical content and tightened oversight of medical marketing, cracking down hard on illicit medical lead generation. An industry that depends heavily on social media to find clients took a significant hit.

The Douyin accounts that Liao Yanqin and Xiao Juan had used to promote their services could not escape throttling and bans. Zhou Ping's company had attracted a good number of clients by running a short-video account, but in May 2023 its Douyin account was silenced for "suspected illegal medical and health advertising." After appealing successfully, the company kept testing ways to avoid another ban and probing the limits of what it could say, while founder Zheng Haiyan began filming her own short videos to share her experience of the work.

The industry's biggest problem right now, in Zhou Ping's view, is an information gap. On one side, demand is large, but many people do not know what a medical companion is, still less where to find one. On the other, platform throttling makes it hard for companions to explain the service to the public, leaving them waiting passively.

And the professional standard of companions roaming the internet varies widely. Liao Yanqin recently came across a short video — "some guy planning to build a companion mini-program, so he took a job to try it out. It had over 900 likes." Watching it made her uncomfortable. "A crowd of amateurs piling in and muddying the water." She has noticed that many companion mini-programs are not run by experienced companions at all. "Anyone can build a platform and sign up part-timers from all over."

Some platforms exercise no oversight whatsoever, with the most cursory vetting when recruiting. After filling in a single form, Deng Mei, a nursing postgraduate at a medical school, found part-time work through a companion platform on Xiaohongshu and began taking orders. The form asked whether she had a medical background and whether she held medical credentials, but no one checked her student ID or anything else, and there was no written test, no interview and no training.

Feng Jin, professor at Fudan University's School of Economics and deputy director of its Center for Employment and Social Security, considers the absence of unified standards a major pain point. National standards, however, inevitably lag somewhat — "and with communication tools as developed as they are, it's hard to stamp out services of uneven quality."

Short of waiting for national regulation, what else can those building companies in this field do? Feng Jin suggests that professional companion firms adopt measures to strengthen internal discipline, set norms for their companions' conduct, and invest in specialist training.

Wang Xuehui, associate research fellow at Fudan University's School of Social Development and Public Policy, says companions have improved many people's experience of seeking care, but that market forces alone fall far short when it comes to serving vulnerable groups. He suggests that grassroots organisations pool resources to offer companion services for older residents. "Neighbourhood committees need a clear picture of the specific medical needs of older people in the community, and should make full use of community volunteers."

Zhejiang and Shanghai have already begun experimenting at that level. In Zhejiang, since 2020 the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University, Zhejiang Hospital and others have introduced non-profit companion services. In February 2023, Pudong New Area People's Hospital opened a free companion hotline and assembled a team linking medical social workers, clinical staff and community volunteers. In September 2023, Shanghai launched its first training programme for elderly-care companions, aimed at staff providing services to older people in the community.

So when does spring arrive for this industry?

Zhou Ping says he will stay in the work. He hopes that official training courses will one day open to companions already working in the market, and that he will be able to earn a state-recognised qualification.

"Raising investment, consolidating revenue, expanding the market" is Zheng Haiyan's wish for the new year. She predicts that 2024 will be the year capital moves into the business.
