As deepfake technology keeps evolving, the old rule that seeing is believing may now be overturned.

"Deepfake" is a portmanteau of "deep learning" and "fake." The most common form today is what is colloquially called "AI face-swapping" — facial images or video forged using deep learning.

Premium subscribers on the social platform X can use Grok, the text-to-image model developed by xAI; a few lines of prompt are enough to generate an animated image of Elon Musk and Donald Trump dancing together. In the livestreaming tool Deep-Live-Cam, a user need only upload a single photograph of a face to swap it, in real time, onto whoever the camera is pointed at. Plenty of software can also synthesise a voice with AI and replace lip movements, making anyone's photograph "speak." For content creators, there is no denying that deepfakes are a useful creative tool.

Yet malicious incidents driven by deepfakes keep coming. In August, South Korea's "Nth Room 2.0" case came to light: offenders had grafted women's faces onto pornographic images and videos and sold them for profit. In September, as Hurricane Helene swept the United States, several AI-generated images of people fleeing the storm circulated alongside conspiracy theories and fabricated news, and were used by some politicians to attack the governing party over its disaster response. And in the current US election, face-swapped photographs and videos starring Trump and Harris continue to spread across social media, inflaming partisan division.

In the World Economic Forum's *Global Risks Report 2024*, AI-generated disinformation was ranked the top global risk of the next two years. The *2024 Artificial Intelligence Security Report* published by Qi An Xin Technology Group noted that deepfake fraud grew by an astonishing 3,000% in 2023. Against this rising tide, many researchers and technology companies are exploring how to use AI models to detect AI-forged imagery — fighting AI with AI.

## Teaching a model to tell real from fake

In AI face-swapped images and videos, there may be something unnatural about the boundary of the face, the movement of the eyes and lips, or the way light falls across the whole face. Early researchers worked from such observations, hand-labelling features — blink rate, for example — and feeding them to an AI detector. But those features tended to work only against a few specific forgery methods, and generalised poorly to new, unknown ones. The common solution today is to train a deep learning model and let it learn for itself how deepfakes differ from real footage in their visual features, and detect forgeries on that basis.

As generative AI models keep improving, however, the surface-level visual flaws in deepfake imagery grow ever fewer. "An important task is to teach the model to extract more robust features," Chen Jingjing, associate professor at Fudan University's School of Computer Science, told *CBN Weekly*.

AI-synthesised footage leaves behind specific traces that the naked eye cannot detect. When a camera captures a photograph or a video, it converts information gathered from the physical world into data with a particular distribution. "But AI-generated images and video have different underlying characteristics — at the level of microscopic detail they may be a string of data distributed differently from genuinely filmed video," He Ting, CTO of Zhongke Ruijian, told *CBN Weekly*.

Zhongke Ruijian was founded in 2020, but its laboratory has nearly two decades of accumulated work in the field. It began researching detection more than a decade ago, when deepfake technology first appeared, and released China's first "AI rumour identification" platform, developing a series of deep learning models to tackle fake news, scam text messages, Photoshopped images and the like. Among them is Ruijian Tuling, a foundation model for forgery detection launched in April 2023, built on a mixture-of-experts architecture. The base model first extracts multimodal, low-level general features that distinguish real from fake; dozens of expert models trained on specific domains then take over — face-swap detection attends only to the facial region, text-to-image and video detection to the global distribution of microscopic features, Photoshop detection to inconsistencies at the edges of altered regions — before a composite judgement is made.

For multimodal deepfakes, Chen Jingjing's research team has this year worked with the digital technology company CETC Financial Technology (CFT) on a reference-assisted multimodal detection method. It uses reference video to learn a rich set of identity-sensitive features — a particular person's manner of speech, linguistic characteristics, facial features — as a baseline, while also mining intrinsic cross-modal inconsistencies, such as a mismatch between lip shape and speech, or between expression and speech.

## Out of the laboratory

A report by the consultancy Deloitte projects that losses from deepfake-related cyberattacks will surge from $12.3 billion in 2023 to $40 billion by 2027, with banks and large enterprises the hardest hit. In February this year, Hong Kong police disclosed a serious AI face-swap fraud: on a conference call at the Hong Kong branch of a British multinational, the fraudsters used face-swapping and voice synthesis to impersonate company executives, claimed the firm was conducting an important secret transaction, and made off with HK$200 million (about 167 million yuan).

Building on its research with Chen Jingjing's team, CFT has launched a multimodal deepfake detection product that uses multimodal content understanding and alignment to estimate the confidence that an image, audio clip or video was genuinely captured, identifying AI-generated content and guarding against identity theft and fraud. CFT says its multimodal tampering detection rate now exceeds 99.9%. The product is currently in its rollout phase, and its key application area is precisely the financial industry — identity verification, anti-fraud for online video calls and similar scenarios.

ZOLOZ, the security technology brand under Ant Digital Technologies, is also developing solutions against deepfake fraud. The team began researching deepfake detection in 2019 and in April this year launched ZOLOZ Deeper, a detection product aimed at business clients. Yao Weibin, ZOLOZ's technical director, told *CBN Weekly* that Deeper not only has powerful algorithms for detecting multimodal deepfakes but is also good at resisting injection attacks on end devices. An injection attack means taking a deepfake video generated in advance and submitting it directly to a facial recognition program by some means that bypasses the camera.

ZOLOZ Deeper currently serves mainly overseas markets, focusing on online identity verification for account opening, payments and the like. "In some Southeast Asian countries, people's identity information isn't networked, so faces and documents are easy to forge, and the proportion of deepfake attacks has even exceeded 10%," Yao said. "Our product can defend against deepfakes at both the system and the algorithm level. After it was deployed in banking scenarios in those countries, the proportion of deepfake attacks fell below 1%."

At the algorithmic level, improving a model's generalisation is key to getting a product into the field. Generalisation refers to a model's ability, once trained, to make accurate detections when applied to new data — put simply, to keep detecting accurately when an entirely new deepfake technique appears.

Detecting deepfakes, Yao said, is a problem of attack and defence: when a new forgery technique arrives, the model has to keep pace and mount a corresponding defence. "Every deepfake model on the market — we have to take it and train and test against it." Ant Digital Technologies' Tianji Lab continually reproduces the various forms deepfake technology takes, and has so far run more than 20,000 "attack-and-defence exercises" against Deeper, helping the detection model iterate.

Beyond finance, Zhongke Ruijian frequently uses its detection products to help public security agencies crack deepfake cases. Its Rui'an intelligent detection platform for deep synthesis, built on the multidimensional Ruijian Tuling model, can identify what type of forgery is present in suspect material, extract traces of the synthesis as evidence, and trace which tools were used to produce it — allowing police to regulate with precision.

Generalisation is also an advantage of the Ruijian Tuling architecture: the foundation model has strong capacity to extract and learn features distinguishing real from fake, so when a new deepfake technique appears, only fine-tuning is needed to restore accurate detection. He Ting said that in February this year, when OpenAI released its text-to-video model Sora, a number of clients approached Zhongke Ruijian to ask whether its existing technology could detect Sora-generated video. "Our base model had never 'seen' video generated by Sora, but our initial detection accuracy still reached over 70%. Sora wasn't open to the public, so we worked only from the few dozen demo videos officially released, fine-tuned the model over a week or two, and accuracy rose above 85%." The Ruijian Tuling model has kept growing: three or four years ago it had only on the order of 100 million parameters; by its official launch last year it had reached 6 billion; this year it has been upgraded past 10 billion.

Moving from the laboratory to the market is not easy. Chen Jingjing noted that when detection models are trained in the lab, the standard datasets used usually target one category of forgery, and the training scenarios are fairly uniform. "But real-world conditions are far more complex — brightness, sharpness, setting and so on can all affect detection accuracy. So between a model from academic research and a deployable product there have to be multiple rounds of fine-tuning and iteration." Beyond common forms such as face-swapping and photo animation, developers also need to stay alert to new variants of deepfakery, such as virtual digital humans.

To raise the standard of deepfake detection products in complex real-world settings, the *Technical Specification for Fake Digital Face Detection in Financial Applications*, co-authored by Ant Digital Technologies, was released at this year's Inclusion Conference on the Bund. It is China's first "AI face-swap" detection standard aimed at financial scenarios, setting out a series of functions detection products should have and quantifying their performance across four dimensions: accuracy, robustness, generalisation and response speed. "We hope it becomes an increasingly scientific standard and sets an example for the industry," Yao said.

South Korea's recent deepfake sex crime cases are a warning that the harms of this technology are seeping into many people's everyday lives, and that anyone could be the next victim.

In 2022 Zhongke Ruijian launched a WeChat mini-program, Ruijian AI, offering ordinary users free detection of AI-generated video, images and text, with the aim of raising public awareness of the risk and prompting people to watch for AI-generated falsehoods around them.

In August this year the company closed a funding round of nearly 100 million yuan, to be used for expanding its business lines and scaling up its detection products. For individual users, it plans to keep the mini-program free while introducing new product types including a software development kit (SDK), an app and end devices.

End devices with built-in deepfake detection have already appeared overseas. In August the antivirus company McAfee partnered with Lenovo to launch Deepfake Detector, billed as the world's first automatic deepfake detector — a local tool deployed on Lenovo AI PCs that can determine in real time, on the device, whether video playing on the computer contains AI-synthesised audio. Internal testing has begun on selected new models in the United States, the United Kingdom and Australia. In an interview, McAfee CTO Steve Grobman explained that most deepfake videos aimed at fraud contain AI-synthesised audio, so starting from the audio can stop the majority of scams in the cradle.

Chen Jingjing agrees that deploying deepfake detection tools on end devices is a viable direction for product innovation. In the case of McAfee's product, "audio forgery isn't as varied as video, and the detection methods are relatively settled, so as a product it doesn't need frequent, large-scale updates. On the other hand, models for detecting forged audio are relatively lightweight and don't demand much compute, so portable devices can run them." It is worth noting that individual consumers tend to seek out one-off detection services only once they have actually been targeted by deepfake fraud, and it remains doubtful whether a product for everyday use can rouse their willingness to pay. "Consumers may be inclined to think that the main responsibility for guarding against deepfakes still lies with the regulator."

The European Union's AI Act, which took effect on 1 August this year, requires providers of AI technology to ensure that synthesised audio, video, text and image content can be detected as AI-generated. On 14 September, the Cyberspace Administration of China released draft *Measures for Labelling AI-Generated Synthetic Content*, proposing that AI-generated content carry both explicit labels (such as text shown to the user) and implicit ones (such as digital watermarks), and setting compliance requirements for content service providers, online content distribution platforms, app distribution platforms and other parties.

Many companies in the field told *CBN Weekly* that fighting deepfakes cannot rest on individual companies or individual products. "All current forms of defence against deepfakes are reactive," Yao Weibin said. "However robust and generalisable a model is, in the face of forgery attacks that change by the day it has to keep iterating — there's no solving this once and for all." Moving from reactive to proactive, he argues, is the key problem the AI detection industry has to confront — and doing so will take legislators, regulators and companies pushing together.
