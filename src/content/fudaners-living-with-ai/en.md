Two years ago, *Fudan Weekly* had a conversation with ChatGPT. GPT-3.5 had just appeared, and AI was already intelligent enough to astonish a good many people.

The AI frenzy ChatGPT set off is still going — DeepSeek is at its height, and Doubao, Kimi, Ernie Bot, Tongyi Qianwen and other AI applications are flourishing. AI tools have moved rapidly into everyday life, and have become an essential study aid on many students' desks. *Fudan Weekly* recently ran a small survey on AI: of the 105 Fudan students who completed it, 80 use AI to help with their studies more than four times a week on average, and 51 use it every day.

On 10 March, in an interview with *Southern Weekly*, Fudan president Jin Li spoke about AI: "We treat it as 'discipline plus tool.' We do reasonably well on the frontier of artificial intelligence. But what I care about more is everyone embracing AI — as a tool, to help you do what you want to do."

What new possibilities has AI opened up in Fudan students' study and daily life? And when AI becomes a study partner, how should we work alongside it?

## Probing the limits of what AI can do

Yuki, a 2023 undergraduate in the School of Journalism, had heard about the various uses of AI tools back in high school. In the year and a half since starting university, AI has turned up in every corner of her studies and daily life.

She has many AI tools installed on her phone and computer, each answering a different need: Doubao for basic questions and English speaking practice; Tongyi Qianwen for recording lectures and summarising them; Diandian, an app under Xiaohongshu, for its strong web search; and she has tried various video generation tools as well.

Faced with the volume of literature her courses require, the tool Yuki uses most is Kimi. "A friend told me Kimi was very good at analysing long texts, so I found a passage and tried it, and it really was useful." She added the Kimi browser extension: "When I hit something I don't understand I just select it with the mouse and I can search and ask the AI right there. English pages can also be translated and summarised with one click — very convenient." Selecting and searching has now become something Yuki does almost unconsciously while reading.

Julie, a 2023 student in the College of Foreign Languages and Literatures, also uses AI to help with reading. She told *Fudan Weekly* that she had joined a neurolinguistics research group, and once needed to produce a literature review on speech and intonation in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). The papers her supervisor recommended came from different disciplines, and both the specialist concepts and the experimental procedures were complex. Facing a maze of a literature base, AI became her navigation.

Julie sent the complex passages from several papers to ChatGPT and asked it to distil a concise framework for the review. "I generally don't feed it a whole paper, because sometimes it doesn't parse accurately, and I think reading the full text myself gives me more of a feel for it and makes it easier to understand." The AI's organising, she says, made her thinking much clearer.

Chen Tianxuan, a 2024 student in the School of Economics, felt lost about how to write when he first took a course assessed by essay, and decided to make AI his judge. After finishing a first draft of a final paper, he uploaded the text to three different AI tools to check for grammatical errors and typos. He then asked them to analyse the paper's strengths and weaknesses. "I give a detailed instruction and have the AI step into a role — 'analyse my article from a reviewer's perspective,' for example. More than 90% of the time, the AI puts its finger right on the problem."

Julie has done something similar. Preparing for TOEFL last October, she typed up her spoken answers and sent them to an AI with the instruction: "I'm preparing for TOEFL. This is my spoken answer to task 1. Please revise it, building on my original answer, improving variety of vocabulary and sentence structure and completeness of logic, so that it reaches a score of 30."

Each new generation of models also widens what people imagine AI can be used for. Zane, a 2022 undergraduate in the School of Computer Science, was among ChatGPT's earliest users. When GPT-3.5 first appeared, a few rounds of conversation were enough to astonish him: the large language model was "completely different from the voice assistants before — very smart." Sending it practice problems from the coding site LeetCode, Zane found the model could "solve nearly every easy problem instantly, and get some of the harder ones right, though at a lower rate."

His favourite model now is o1, which OpenAI released in September 2024. Compared with earlier generations, o1 is particularly good at breaking a complex problem into sub-problems and reasoning through them, a leap in capability for data analysis, mathematics and programming.

Early this year, Zane experienced that strength first-hand at the Mathematical Contest in Modeling (MCM). A three-person team he led had four and a half days to build a mathematical model for a complex real-world problem, compute an optimal solution, and write a paper of more than twenty pages. The contest permits AI tools, and for this battle he bought a new ChatGPT membership specifically — 50 conversations a week with o1. "Honestly I didn't believe at first that o1 could solve it. I just sent the problem statement to see what it would say." To his surprise, o1 produced a very complete framework that laid a foundation for the paper. "We studied it closely and found the framework entirely reasonable, and in the end we just built our own research on top of it."

In this collaboration between human and machine intelligence, o1 is a near-omniscient assistant while the human is more like the one steering the whole operation. "The main role of the human brain is understanding the model, setting the parameters, and making sure the model is feasible. On parameter setting and data acquisition, for instance, if our mathematics isn't good enough to understand what o1 generates, we obviously can't go on," Zane said.

## Finding AI's two sides

This was Zane's second MCM. A year earlier, GPT models' reasoning was still weak and could only assist: "You'd give it a model you'd roughly designed and ask what could be optimised, how to add more 'advanced' elements."

But he has realised that AI is profoundly changing how he comes to know new things. "I couldn't come up with those 'advanced' elements myself, and teaching myself would take a long time. With AI I can find out quickly what they are and how to use them. Now, whenever I want to get up to speed on a new concept, I'm certain to turn to AI." In his view, AI can take him into his own blind spots through easily understood conversation in a very short time, opening up a world he hasn't seen before.

Compared with a search engine, AI filters out ads and redundancy and outputs what the user needs quickly and directly. For basic questions that come up while studying — synonyms for English speaking practice, the structure of C++ code — Julie says AI has essentially replaced the search engine.

Yuki feels the same: "A lot of the time I just want to acquire knowledge, not create something new. AI is enough for that."

How to acquire knowledge efficiently is itself a skill for AI users. Damokelisi Luzi, a 2023 doctoral student in the School of Life Sciences, told *Fudan Weekly* that AI has taught her how to ask. "Writing prompts has taught me to break a question or a goal into more specific modules, or express it more simply and directly, which gives me that grounded sense of needing to be clear about what my next step is. Now, whatever I'm asking, I instinctively express it in more detail and more fully."

"Prompt" was Youdao Dictionary's word of the year for 2023, and prompt engineer has become an emerging profession. Fang Shixin, assistant research fellow at Fudan's Institute of Higher Education, told *Fudan Weekly*: "You could say the prompt you put in largely determines the quality of what AI produces."

In her experience, prompts built on chain-of-thought improve the quality of AI-generated content. "You have to talk to AI the way you'd assign work to another person. If you simply tell ChatGPT 'summarise this lecture for me,' the summary it writes may not match what you need. You have to be clear about what kind of lecture it was, who the summary is for, in what tone and at what length, which points to emphasise — adding constraints to the output step by step."

For Fang, AI is already indispensable to teaching and research, not only generating basic material like text summaries and suggested revisions, but sparking ideas over successive rounds of conversation and supplying fresh angles for lesson preparation and research.

Yet AI tools have an A-side that opens up thinking and, sometimes, a B-side that disrupts it.

AI greatly improved Zane's efficiency at the MCM, but he says, "my own thinking gets carried along with it." He jokes that o1's approach is "modelling in prose": the framework is exceptionally complete, yet it can't solve the real problem because of missing data and other issues. All he could do was fine-tune the model on top of o1's framework — because once you have seen that highly idealised structure, it is very hard to think your way out of it.

Damokelisi Luzi has found that AI's answers occasionally fall into going round in circles. Once she tried using AI to help outline a research project; she was very satisfied with its first response, but when she raised more specific, more detailed suggestions and objections, "the AI was easily led by the nose."

Large models are trained by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), optimising output by learning from human responses. The goal of that training is to generate content that fits language patterns and user expectations. In other words, its output is based on probability, not on logic. This is what produces the people-pleasing behaviour described above.

"If I tell it 'the answer you gave me is wrong,' it goes along with me and changes the answer to something arbitrary," Chen Tianxuan told *Fudan Weekly*. "Several times it has even given me answers that contradicted each other."

The phenomenon he describes can be summed up in the term "hallucination." Probability-based output makes large language models highly prone to generating content that doesn't match real data. Chen also says AI often generates fabricated references, leaving him feeling deceived. It has made him realise that cross-checking information and judging truth from falsehood keenly matter especially in the age of AI.

Fang Shixin notes that even the smartest large language model hallucinates plenty. "Ask it the same question ten times and it may give you ten different answers. So I think students need to understand this characteristic clearly, and not look up to AI so much that they treat its output as gospel."

## Locating AI's place in the scheme of things

AI cannot always meet every human requirement, but some students have found, through continual adjustment to these tools, that they are close to being unable to do without them.

Julie once took a data structures course from the computer science department, and would often have AI explain complex principles and fix code she had written wrong. As one of the few students from another department in the class, facing unfamiliar concepts in lectures, she chose to lean on AI. "When I'm learning material that's genuinely hard for me, without AI's help I might lose confidence in my ability to learn at all."

"How dependent on AI are people really" is becoming a topic of online debate. On 11 December 2024, OpenAI had a large-scale outage, and a joke circulated on social media that global research had stalled for four hours. In recent months, some media have reported that many university students have developed an AI dependency — using AI out of laziness and copying large blocks of generated text straight into assignments and papers.

*Fudan Weekly* found that the students interviewed worried to varying degrees that AI might weaken their capacity for independent learning, but none relied on the tools wholesale.

Chen Tianxuan was once curious what a paper written entirely by AI would look like, but came away disappointed. "An AI-generated article has essentially no academic value — it lacks substance and depth of thought, and has nothing original in it. As a first-year, what I write isn't much better, but I think a piece of writing has to contain your own thinking." TJ, a 2023 undergraduate in the Department of History, also told *Fudan Weekly* that AI-generated language is stiff and makes her uneasy to read, and that papers it writes are far below her own level. "So I only use AI as a search engine, to give me ideas."

"If students' use of AI is within reasonable bounds and doesn't touch academic or examination integrity, my attitude is relatively open." Damokelisi Luzi has worked as a teaching assistant for undergraduate lab courses, and in her observation most students use AI only for support work like answering basic questions and searching literature; their assignments don't have a heavy AI flavour.

On 28 November 2024, Fudan's Office of Academic Affairs issued *Fudan University Provisions on the Use of AI Tools in Undergraduate Theses (Designs) (Trial)*, the first regulatory document at a Chinese university aimed specifically at AI tools in undergraduate theses and design projects. It sets out "six prohibitions" barring AI from core stages including research design, data analysis and writing. When using AI tools, students must also explicitly disclose the tool's name and version number, when it was used, for what specific purpose, what the tool generated or suggested, and exactly where in the thesis it was used. Communication University of China, Tianjin University of Science and Technology, Fuzhou University, Hubei University and others subsequently issued similar rules.

Fang Shixin argues that the goal, as more universities issue AI policies, is not to eliminate AI but to build students' basic AI literacy and their ability to use it sensibly.

A 2024 paper from a University of Pennsylvania research team, *Generative AI Can Harm Learning*, illustrates vividly why regulating how these tools are used is necessary. The study, conducted at a high school in Turkey, divided participants into three groups: a control group not allowed to use AI at all; one experimental group with unrestricted access to ChatGPT; and another with access to GPT Tutor, specially designed to preserve students' independent learning. In the short term, both experimental groups scored markedly higher in mathematics than the control, with the GPT Tutor group improving most. But on the closed-book final exam, students with unrestricted ChatGPT access scored significantly lower than the control group, while the GPT Tutor group matched it.

In practice, universities' regulation of AI tools and their guidance on using them are two sides of the same thing. Fudan is currently building an AI-BEST curriculum, with 116 AI-related courses launched in the 2024–25 academic year. These courses teach not only the technical principles of AI but how AI is transforming research and practice across disciplines, helping students build technical literacy.

AI-enabled teaching and learning is beginning to make life easier for staff and students alike. According to the academic affairs office, this semester every course on Fudan's online education platform has been connected to DeepSeek-R1, giving more than a thousand online courses an AI teaching assistant that can answer questions. *Fudan Weekly* reporters also found that students can now access a locally deployed DeepSeek-R1 671B model through the ehall online services portal.

"As teachers, we have a responsibility to bring students the newest ideas. We want Fudan's students to be better at commanding AI," Fang Shixin said. "I think people with critical and independent thinking can make fuller use of AI and improve their efficiency far more; conversely, people who lack autonomy and inner drive may not use AI wisely, so their efficiency doesn't improve at all, or even falls. The gap that already existed between these two kinds of people will widen because of AI, until it becomes a chasm."

As human-AI coexistence becomes the trend, Fudan students are choosing to explore AI's ideal role rationally and imaginatively, and to locate its place in their studies and their lives.

Zane, who works with code every day, once lamented when he first encountered AI that his IQ seemed to have dropped — AI effortlessly wrote the fiddly syntactic details perfectly, leaving him unable to master the corresponding knowledge solidly. In response he has deliberately increased how much he thinks independently: "For some code I'll ask AI once, and the second time I know how to write it, so I try not to use AI. Code the AI outputs, I type out myself — if you just copy it, you learn nothing. I like asking AI questions while learning new things from its reasoning and its answers."

"I think AI's translation is sometimes already better than ours as English majors." Julie occasionally feels a sense of crisis. Working out how not to be replaced is one reason she began taking computer science courses and joined the neurolinguistics group.

But she also knows that AI cannot fully grasp subtle human emotion and cultural expression, and that high-level simultaneous interpretation at diplomatic occasions and international conferences still cannot do without professional human interpreters. "AI has made me realise how important it is to sharpen professional skill, especially the ability to communicate across cultures. On the other hand, dividing the work with AI matters too — for assisted reading, text summarising, even learning to code, I need to make full use of how immediate, accurate and cheap AI is to improve my own ability and productivity."

Fang Shixin's message to students: "When you have love for something and inner drive, you will want to understand and explore what you're doing more deeply, and that's when AI can bring you to a deeper understanding of a field faster. Why you use a tool, and how you use it, matter enormously."

Amid the turnover of technology, the ways AI can support study and life still hold endless possibilities. What shines constantly, though, is the human impulse to ask why, and the persistence to work out how. In living alongside AI, we may leap toward the stars more lightly, and touch answers once out of reach.
