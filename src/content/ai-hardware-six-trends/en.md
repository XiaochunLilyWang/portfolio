The past year has been a year of explosion in consumer AI hardware.

Last February, EssilorLuxottica, Ray-Ban's parent company, shared good news on its earnings call: cumulative sales of the AI glasses it makes with Meta had reached 2 million pairs. That was an unexpected figure, given how fresh the memory still was of Google Glass, VR headsets and other eyewear hardware coming to nothing. More unexpected still, over the following year the same glasses sold another 7 million pairs.

Compared with their predecessors, the most important functional change in Ray-Ban's AI glasses is the AI assistant. It can translate, reply to messages, take and make calls, and answer all sorts of questions like a ChatGPT you carry around. It can also call on the camera in the glasses, using AI image recognition to tell you what building you're looking at — and record a video while it's at it.

The improvement in AI capability is the main force behind this wave of AI hardware. When AI is smart enough and cheap enough, carrying around a device whose only function is AI no longer seems like a burden. With the recent craze for OpenClaw, for instance, people have even assembled a Mac Mini plus portable Wi-Fi plus a power bank purely to keep it with them. When AI is genuinely useful, users turn out to be less averse to inconvenience than you'd think.

So over the past year AI hardware has stopped fixating on the dramatic Humane AI Pin script of ending the smartphone. Instead, devices like Plaud — which reopened the office market with a plain recording card — have become the mainstream. They are working to bring the model down out of the cloud and into glasses, earbuds, pendants, rings and recording cards, sitting more comfortably alongside the user, perched on the bridge of the nose and the curve of the ear, becoming the large model's acute senses.

## ① Recording hardware is no longer just a typist

Five or six years ago, iFlytek and others had already released recording hardware with AI in it, but the AI of the time could at most transcribe a recording into text, producing no increment in information or value.

The new generation of recording cards, led by Plaud, no longer records mechanically — it understands what people are talking about. It writes its own meeting summaries and notes down what needs doing. You can also ask it directly about details in the recording. They have gone from being a typist to an assistant with suggestions.

## ② Letting hardware open its eyes

Besides being smarter, the other major characteristic of this wave of AI models is multimodality, meaning they handle not only text but visual and auditory information.

So more and more AI hardware comes with a camera. AI glasses with cameras can tell the user through voice interaction what the thing in front of them is, or which restaurants are in a given building, and can also answer questions, navigate and make payments. Beyond that, smart cameras like BodyPark ATOM, which recognises and corrects exercise form, and pendants like Rex.fit The Drop, which photographs food automatically as the user eats and counts calories, have moved from concept to product.

## ③ Becoming the phone's helper

Hardware startups in 2025 rarely set the radical goal of replacing the smartphone. They aim more pragmatically at filling the phone's dead zones — using hardware to do what the phone can't do, or can't do well, because of its physical form, privacy policies or usage context.

The Plaud Note recording card, the first to break out, addresses a simple pain point: the iPhone can't record call audio. Shaped like a card, it attaches magnetically to the back of the phone and uses a vibration-conduction sensor to pick up the body vibration as the earpiece sounds, bypassing the operating system's restrictions on third-party recording software. Plaud's other recording device, NotePin, is a round clip that can attach magnetically to clothing or be worn as a pendant, removing the step of taking out the phone to start recording.

Some AI glasses makers have gone after the blind spot of first-person perspective. A phone camera shoots high-quality images, but it always needs a hand to hold it up, and its physical position makes it hard to replicate the field of view of the human eye. Glasses built around photography and video from brands such as TCL RayNeo, Xiaomi and Quark are like a camera growing on your face, convenient for recording life while exercising, cooking or in other situations where taking out a phone is awkward.

## ④ From waiting for orders to finding work

Beyond physically freeing the hands, the deeper change in AI hardware is in interaction logic. It is no longer a tool silently awaiting instructions but is gradually acquiring initiative. Compared with traditional hardware like smart speakers, 2025's AI hardware can sense environmental context and judge for itself when to step in.

Looki, an AI camera in pendant form, no longer waits mechanically for a shutter command but decides its own capture frequency based on the information density of the environment: wear it around a trade show, where there is a lot going on, and it records frequently; if you're driving and the view hasn't changed in half an hour, it judges there is nothing worth recording and stops. iFlytek's AI earbuds sense the audio stream and, on detecting that a meeting or call has started, proactively ask whether to begin recording — a shift from people seeking service to service seeking people. The smart pendant from Limitless, recently acquired by Meta, records all day, assembles daily insights from the audio, and proactively reminds the user of schedules and tasks that came up.

## ⑤ Trading imperceptibility for an all-day pass

"Imperceptible" has been the most frequently used phrase in AI hardware user experience over the past year. The AI recording bean jointly launched by Anker and Feishu leads on imperceptible wear: it weighs only 10 grams and is just 23.2 millimetres across, worn as a collar clip or a pendant. The Oura AI ring dispenses with screens and buttons altogether, and has no vibration or light alerts either. Its CEO has said this is because any form of active interruption breaks the sense of the device merging with the body, and so shortens how long users wear it.

These trade-offs all serve the goal of letting glasses, earbuds and rings continuously record the user's vital signs, what they see and what they hear. Only when the user keeps the device on, accumulating enough data, does the AI's intelligence have anything to work with.

Behind this is the replacement of the GUI by voice and sensory interaction: AI hardware is pushing us into a post-screen era. And this all-day, frictionless collection of data from the physical world also underpins the restructuring of business models that follows.

## ⑥ The money shifts from selling hardware to selling services

As AI occupies an ever more central position, hardware grows ever more peripheral. The new wave of AI hardware companies no longer want to make money from selling machines alone; they would rather sell services.

Plaud's recording card, besides the hardware, comes with three membership tiers: standard members get 300 minutes of transcription a month; the professional subscription costs 339 a year and raises that to 1,200 minutes; the premium tier, at 1,099 a year, gives unlimited transcription.

The Oura smart ring has made the same choice. The ring itself costs $349 to $499, while the software subscription is $5.99 a month or $69.99 a year, with the AI health coach Oura Advisor offering personalised health management suggestions based on the user's health goals, preferences and even life events. In a recent interview, the company's CEO said the subscription model is necessary to support the company's focus on health insights, and that users' continued subscriptions create long-term value.
